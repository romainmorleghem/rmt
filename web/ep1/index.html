Random Matrices · Part 1

Random Matrices: Order from Noise

Random matrix theory is a happy marriage between probability and linear algebra. Fill a large symmetric matrix with random numbers: its eigenvalues are anything but random. They repel each other, they behave like a gas of electric charges, and they pile up into a perfect semicircle. And the same statistics appear in heavy nuclei, chaotic billiards and the zeros of the Riemann zeta function.

1What is a random matrix?

A real symmetric $N\times N$ matrix $H$ ($H_{ij}=H_{ji}$) has $N$ real eigenvalues $\lambda_1\le\dots\le\lambda_N$, the numbers for which $Hv=\lambda v$ has a non-zero solution $v$: its spectrum. Draw the entries at random and the eigenvalues become random variables, strongly correlated with each other. Random matrix theory asks: where do they lie (their density), and how far apart are neighbours (their spacings)?

Left: the matrix as an image (gold positive, blue negative). Right: the histogram of its eigenvalues. Increase $N$ to watch the histogram converge; change the law of the entries to see that the limit does not care.

Where it shows up.

  • 1928, John Wishart: statistics; the sample covariance matrix of a data table.
  • 1950s, Eugene Wigner: energy levels of heavy nuclei (uranium, thorium). Too complex to compute one by one, so replace the Hamiltonian by a random symmetric matrix and predict the statistics of the levels; the spacings of measured neutron resonances agree.
  • 1962, Freeman Dyson: the three symmetry classes ($\beta=1,2,4$) and the Coulomb-gas picture.
  • 1972, Hugh Montgomery & Dyson: the zeros of the Riemann zeta function have the pair statistics of complex Hermitian random matrices; checked by Andrew Odlyzko on billions of zeros.
  • 1984, Bohigas, Giannoni, Schmit: a quantum system whose classical motion is chaotic has random-matrix level statistics; a regular (integrable) one has independent levels.
  • Disordered metals, wave chaos in optics and acoustics, wireless channels, finance, neural networks…

2Which random matrix? The Gaussian ensembles

Two natural wishes. Independent entries: each entry is drawn on its own (easy to simulate). Rotation invariance: no basis is preferred, $P(OHO^{\mathsf T})=P(H)$ for every rotation $O$ (in physics, the basis in which we write a Hamiltonian is arbitrary).

Why only Gaussian? (the $2\times2$ case, à la Maxwell)

1. $H=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\b&d\end{pmatrix}$: three random numbers.
2. Independence: $P(H)=f(a)\,g(b)\,f(d)$ (the same $f$ for both diagonal entries, since swapping the two basis vectors is a rotation).
3. Invariance: $P$ depends only on the eigenvalues, i.e. on $\mathrm{Tr}\,H=a+d$ and $\mathrm{Tr}\,H^2=a^2+d^2+2b^2$ (these two numbers fix both eigenvalues).
4. Taking logs: $\ln f(a)+\ln g(b)+\ln f(d)=\phi(a+d,\ a^2+d^2+2b^2)$. Varying $b$ alone changes only $\mathrm{Tr}H^2$, through $2b^2$; so $\partial_2\phi=\frac{(\ln g)'(b)}{4b}$ depends on $b$ only, yet is a function of $(\mathrm{Tr}H,\mathrm{Tr}H^2)$ which can be changed independently of $b$: it is a constant $-c$.
5. Then $\ln f(a)+\ln f(d)=-c(a^2+d^2)+\psi(a+d)$, which forces $\psi$ to be linear. Hence $P(H)\propto e^{-c\,\mathrm{Tr}H^2+k\,\mathrm{Tr}H}$: Gaussian entries (shift $H$ to remove $k$).

This is James Clerk Maxwell's 1860 argument for gas velocities: independent components + no preferred direction ⟹ Gaussian.

Rotate the basis: the entries of $OHO^{\mathsf T}$ change, but the eigenvalues, $\mathrm{Tr}H$ and $\mathrm{Tr}H^2$ do not. A rotation-invariant law can only depend on these.

Three flavours, $\beta=1,2,4$. Depending on the symmetries of the physical system, the matrix is real symmetric (time-reversal symmetry: GOE, $\beta=1$), complex Hermitian (broken time reversal, e.g. a magnetic field: GUE, $\beta=2$) or quaternion self-dual (time reversal with half-integer spin: GSE, $\beta=4$). $\beta$ is the number of real parameters in each off-diagonal entry. All three: $$P(H)\;\propto\;\exp\Big(-\frac{\beta N}{4}\,\mathrm{Tr}\,H^2\Big),$$ the factor $N$ keeping the spectrum in $[-2,2]$.

3Level repulsion and universality

Top: 60 independent uniform points. Bottom: 60 consecutive eigenvalues of a $400\times400$ GOE matrix, unfolded to the same mean spacing. Independent points clump; eigenvalues repel.

Independent points. If points are thrown independently with density 1, the probability that a gap exceeds $s$ is the probability that an interval of length $s$ is empty, $e^{-s}$; the gap density is $P(s)=e^{-s}$, largest at $s=0$.

Eigenvalues, the $2\times2$ proof. Solving the quadratic equation, $$\lambda_\pm=\frac{a+d}{2}\pm\frac12\sqrt{(a-d)^2+(2b)^2},\qquad s=\lambda_+-\lambda_-=\sqrt{(a-d)^2+(2b)^2}.$$ The spacing is the distance to the origin of the point $(a-d,\,2b)$. For GOE this point is an isotropic Gaussian cloud, so the probability of a spacing $s$ is (length of the ring of radius $s$) × (Gaussian weight): $$P(s)\propto 2\pi s\,e^{-s^2/4}\quad\xrightarrow{\ \text{mean }1\ }\quad P_{\rm GOE}(s)=\frac{\pi s}{2}\,e^{-\pi s^2/4}\qquad\text{(Wigner surmise)}.$$ A small spacing needs two coincidences ($a=d$ and $b=0$). For $\beta=2$, $b$ is complex: the point lives in 3D, the ring becomes a sphere of area $4\pi s^2$, and $P(s)\propto s^2e^{-cs^2}$; for $\beta=4$, $s^4$. In general $P(s)\propto s^\beta$ near 0.

Each dot is one random $2\times2$ matrix, placed at $(a-d,\,2b)$ (for β = 2 we show the projection of a 3D cloud). The fraction of matrices inside the ring is the probability of that spacing; the histogram of distances builds the Wigner surmise.

Large matrices and universality. The surmise comes from $2\times2$ matrices, yet it describes the bulk spacings of large matrices to about 1 %. And it does not depend on the law of the entries (coin flips work just as well), only on the symmetry class $\beta$.

4The joint density and the Coulomb gas

Write $H=O\Lambda O^{\mathsf T}$ (eigenvalues $\Lambda$, rotation $O$) and change variables from the entries to $(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_N,O)$. The Gaussian weight only involves $\mathrm{Tr}H^2=\sum_i\lambda_i^2$. The Jacobian is the ring again: for $2\times2$, $(a-d,\,2b)=(s\cos2\theta,\ s\sin2\theta)$ are polar coordinates with radius $s=\lambda_1-\lambda_2$, so $da\,db\,dd\propto|\lambda_1-\lambda_2|\,d\lambda_1d\lambda_2d\theta$. For $N$ eigenvalues every pair contributes: $$P(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_N)\;\propto\;\prod_{iReading (Dyson): $N$ charges on a line, held by a harmonic spring, repelling through $-\ln r$, the electrostatic energy in a two-dimensional world (force $\propto1/r$), at temperature $1/\beta$.

Live Langevin dynamics of the Coulomb gas: $d\lambda_i=\big(-\tfrac{\lambda_i}{2}+\tfrac1N\sum_{j\ne i}\tfrac{1}{\lambda_i-\lambda_j}\big)dt+\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\beta N}}\,dB_i$. Its stationary law is exactly the joint density above. The histogram is averaged over time. Freeze it: the charges stop at the zeros of the Hermite polynomial $H_N$ (Stieltjes, 1885).

5The semicircle, step by step

We derive Wigner's law $\rho(x)=\frac1{2\pi}\sqrt{4-x^2}$ from the Coulomb gas, with three tools: a principal value, the Sokhotski–Plemelj formula (Gauss's law) and Liouville's theorem.

Step 1 · Continuum limit. Describe the charges by a density $\rho(x)$ with $\int\rho=1$: $\sum_i f(\lambda_i)\to N\int f\rho$ and $\sum_{i
Step 2 · Force balance. A charge at $x$ feels the spring $-x/2$ and the push of all the others. At equilibrium, for every $x$ in the support, $$\frac x2=\mathrm{P}\!\!\int\frac{\rho(y)}{x-y}\,dy .$$ Tool 1 · principal value. $\mathrm{P}\!\int$ removes a small symmetric window around $y=x$ and lets it shrink: a charge does not push itself. (Equivalently: $\frac{\delta S}{\delta\rho}=\frac{x^2}{4}-\int\ln|x-y|\rho(y)dy$ is constant on the support, the condition of a conductor in equilibrium; differentiate in $x$.)
Step 3 · The field in the complex plane. For $z=x+iy$ off the line define $$G(z)=\int\frac{\rho(y)}{z-y}\,dy .$$ In two dimensions the field of a unit charge at $y$ is $\frac{z-y}{|z-y|^2}=\overline{\big(\tfrac{1}{z-y}\big)}$: the electric field is $\overline{G(z)}$. $G$ is complex-differentiable off the support and $G(z)\approx 1/z$ far away.
Step 4 · Crossing the wire. Tool 2 · Sokhotski–Plemelj. $\displaystyle\frac{1}{x-y\pm i0}=\mathrm{P}\frac{1}{x-y}\mp i\pi\,\delta(x-y)$, hence $$G(x\pm i0)=\mathrm{P}\!\!\int\frac{\rho(y)}{x-y}dy\;\mp\;i\pi\rho(x).$$ Physically: the horizontal field is continuous across the wire (it is the force of Step 2), the vertical field jumps by $2\pi\rho(x)$ (Gauss's law: the flux out of a small box equals the charge inside).
Step 5 · The trick. Adding the two sides and using Step 2: $G(x+i0)+G(x-i0)=x$. Put $F(z)=G(z)-\frac z2$: then $F(x+i0)=-F(x-i0)$ on the support, so $F(x+i0)^2=F(x-i0)^2$: $F^2$ has no jump; it is complex-differentiable in the whole plane (its singularities at the edges are removable). Far away $F=-\frac z2+\frac1z+O(z^{-2})$, so $F^2=\frac{z^2}{4}-1+O(1/z)$.
Step 6 · Liouville. Tool 3 · Liouville's theorem. A function complex-differentiable in the whole plane and bounded is constant; one growing like a polynomial is a polynomial. Applied to $F^2-\big(\frac{z^2}{4}-1\big)$, which tends to 0: it is identically 0. So $F=-\frac12\sqrt{z^2-4}$ (branch $\approx -z/2$ at infinity) and $$G(z)=\frac{z-\sqrt{z^2-4}}{2},\qquad \rho(x)=-\frac1\pi\,\mathrm{Im}\,G(x+i0)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{4-x^2}\ \ (|x|\le2),$$ and $\rho=0$ for $|x|>2$, where the root is real. The support $[-2,2]$ is an output, not an input.

The field $\overline{G(z)}$ of the semicircle charge (gold) on the wire. Hover near the wire: just above, $G=\frac x2-i\pi\rho(x)$; just below, $G=\frac x2+i\pi\rho(x)$. The horizontal part equals $x/2$ (force balance), the vertical part flips and its jump is $2\pi\rho$.

6Any β: from a Poisson gas to a crystal

The Coulomb gas makes sense for any $\beta>0$. Dumitriu and Edelman (2002) found a matrix model for all of them: the symmetric tridiagonal matrix with diagonal $\mathcal N(0,2)$ and off-diagonal $\chi_{\beta(N-1)},\chi_{\beta(N-2)},\dots,\chi_\beta$ (all divided by $\sqrt2$) has exactly the joint density $\prod|\lambda_i-\lambda_j|^\beta e^{-\sum\lambda_i^2/2}$. Only $O(N)$ numbers: large $N$ in the browser.

Left: eigenvalue density (always a semicircle). Right: bulk spacings with the reference curves (Poisson, GOE, GUE, GSE). Bottom: 50 consecutive unfolded eigenvalues. Small β: almost independent; large β: a crystal.

7Our computations

Quantum billiards: regular vs chaotic

700 eigenvalues of the Laplacian (a vibrating membrane) in a quarter circle (exact Bessel zeros) and a quarter stadium (5-point finite differences), Dirichlet boundary, unfolded with Weyl's law. The circle (integrable) gives independent levels, the stadium (chaotic) gives GOE repulsion: the Bohigas–Giannoni–Schmit conjecture.

Anderson transition: metal or insulator

Electron on a $14^3$ cubic lattice, hopping 1, random site energies in $[-W/2,W/2]$; spacings at the band centre, 8 disorder realisations per $W$. The statistics slide from GOE (metal, extended states) to Poisson (insulator, localised states) around $W_c\approx16.5$.

Zeros of the Riemann zeta function

The first 10 000 non-trivial zeros $\frac12+it_n$ (computed with mpmath), unfolded with $N(t)=\frac{t}{2\pi}\ln\frac{t}{2\pi e}+\frac78$. Their spacings follow GUE ($\beta=2$).

A very rare event: all eigenvalues positive

For GOE, $P(\lambda_{\min}>0)\approx e^{-\theta N^2}$ with $\theta=\frac{\ln3}{4}\approx0.2747$ (Dean and Majumdar, 2006). The "rigged coin" of Rare Events (sample $H+cI$ and re-weight by $e^{-\frac N4(2c\,\mathrm{Tr}H-Nc^2)}$) works up to $N\approx6$, then the weights explode. A "moving wall" (Metropolis simulation of the Coulomb gas, pushing a hard wall from $-2.6$ to $0$ in $\approx0.4N^2$ steps and multiplying the conditional probabilities) reaches $N=30$. Why $N^2$: squeezing the whole gas costs an energy of order $N^2$.
